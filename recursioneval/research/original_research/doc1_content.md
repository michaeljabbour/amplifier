Evaluating Recursive Reasoning in LLMs: Frameworks and an Amplifier-Focused Benchmark
Introduction
Recursion – the ability to solve problems by breaking them into self-similar subproblems – is a fundamental aspect of both algorithmic thinking and complex reasoning. However, evaluating how well large language models (LLMs) handle recursive tasks (in code generation or in logical reasoning) remains challenging. Many LLMs struggle with deeply nested logic or tasks requiring repeated self-references, often losing track of goals or introducing errors as the chain of reasoning grows[1][2]. Amplifier, a modular AI coding/automation system with a “stable kernel” and extensible sub-agents, provides an opportunity to mitigate these issues by structuring the model’s workflow[3][4]. In this report, we survey existing benchmarks and methods related to recursion in LLMs, and then design a comprehensive eval benchmark tailored for Amplifier. This benchmark will test multiple forms of recursion – from algorithmic code recursion (e.g. computing Fibonacci or tree traversals) to recursive reasoning and planning (nested logic puzzles, multi-step plans), as well as self-referential prompt chaining (the model calling itself or its outputs repeatedly). We define the hypothesis, tasks, metrics, and provide runnable scaffolds (using an evaluation harness) to implement these tests. The goal is to ensure the eval is modular and extensible, so it can be applied across different model types (code-focused or general-purpose LLMs) and easily integrated into Amplifier’s pipeline.
Hypothesis: By leveraging structured workflows and tools (as in Amplifier), an LLM can achieve more reliable performance on deeply recursive tasks – maintaining correctness and minimizing hallucinations even as recursion depth increases – compared to a baseline model without such support. We expect Amplifier’s features like persistent memory, sub-agent decomposition, and context-management to help mitigate the known failure modes of LLMs in recursive reasoning (such as context drift and compounding errors[5][6]).
Challenges of Recursion in LLMs
Recursive tasks quickly expose the limits of an LLM’s reasoning depth and consistency. Algorithmic recursion (e.g. computing the nth Fibonacci number or traversing a tree) often requires the model to either know the mathematical pattern or simulate a step-by-step procedure. While LLMs have memorized short sequences, they can falter on longer sequences without tool use. For example, when prompted to iteratively generate Fibonacci numbers, GPT-3.5 sometimes produced incorrect values after a few steps (skipping or miscalculating a number)[7]. In one experiment, a recursive prompt was set up so that the model’s output became the next input (simulating a function calling itself); although the model knew the Fibonacci sequence in principle, it started making mistakes as the chain grew, outputting 2504 where the correct 18th Fibonacci number is 2584[8]. This illustrates how hallucination or error can creep into a recursive chain and then propagate. A major challenge is detecting and correcting such errors mid-chain – an area of active research (e.g. using the model to fact-check itself or using external tools to verify steps[9]).
In general-purpose reasoning, deeply nested logic or multi-step plans can cause “context rot”, where the model gradually loses sight of earlier facts or goals as the prompt grows[10][11]. Anthropic has described this phenomenon as models getting “dumber” over long conversations, as important context scrolls out of the window[10]. Even with chain-of-thought prompting, long reasoning chains can drift off-course. For instance, sequential reasoning methods like basic CoT, ReAct, or Reflexion (where the model reflects on errors) work well for short problems, but on long-horizon tasks they often degrade – early steps are forgotten or “drift” out of context, leading to goal misalignment and failure loops[6]. In a recent planning benchmark, pure sequential prompting led to recurrent failures as the task length increased, due to loss of high-level goals from the prompt history[12][5]. Clearly, maintaining global coherence across recursive steps is difficult for current models.
Another issue is semantic drift vs. factual accuracy when outputs are chained. Interestingly, a 2025 study on recursive summarization found that factual details remain mostly intact across multiple generations, but the semantic intent can drift dramatically[1][2]. After 10 recursive generations of GPT-4 outputs (each time feeding the output back in), factual accuracy dropped only ~2%, yet the purpose or meaning of the text shifted by over 40% (measured by a “Purpose Fidelity” metric)[13]. For example, an academic excerpt about Descartes morphed into a generic leadership lesson after many re-phrasings[14]. This kind of drift means that even if no blatant hallucination occurs, the output can lose its original intent under deep chaining – a subtle failure mode important to evaluate[15]. An effective recursion-capable system should preserve not just factual bits but also the overall meaning/goal throughout the chain.
Depth vs. reliability trade-off: As tasks require more recursive steps, models often hit a wall. A recent evaluation by Apple (“The Illusion of Thinking”) tested advanced reasoning-focused LLMs on puzzles like the Tower of Hanoi, which inherently require a recursive solution strategy[16]. They found that for a modest number of disks the models could produce many correct moves, but beyond a certain puzzle size the accuracy plummeted to zero – even models with step-by-step reasoning could not scale to the exponential complexity[17][18]. Notably, even when given an explicit known algorithm for the task, the LLMs still failed at similar depths[19]. For example, a model might handle ~100 moves of Hanoi correctly (around 7–8 disks) but completely break down by 10 disks, or solve a simpler planning puzzle but fail a slightly more complex variant[20]. This suggests current LLMs have a recursion depth limit beyond which they either give up or start making large mistakes. Indeed, the Apple study observed that models would sometimes “give up” early – using fewer reasoning tokens than allowed once the problem got too hard, indicating a fundamental limitation in their recursive reasoning ability[21][22].
Finally, hallucination can compound in recursive processes. If an early step in a reasoning chain introduces a false assumption, later steps build on it, leading the final answer far off track. Techniques like self-consistency (generating multiple independent reasoning paths and voting) and self-reflection (having the model critique and correct its previous answer) have been proposed to combat this. For example, the Reflexion approach lets an agent revise its plan after detecting an error, which improved accuracy on some tasks by breaking the naive linear chain and adding a feedback loop[6]. Another recent method, DR-CoT (Dynamic Recursive Chain-of-Thought), integrates recursive reasoning with a voting mechanism to mitigate errors from any single chain[23][24]. By truncating less relevant context and aggregating multiple reasoning attempts, DR-CoT improved reasoning accuracy on challenging benchmarks (like math word problems and code generation) by a few percentage points even for smaller models[25][26]. These efforts underscore that handling recursion isn’t just about pushing the model to think longer, but also about managing context and combining multiple tries to avoid the pitfalls of a single hallucinated chain.
Why Amplifier can help: Amplifier’s design specifically targets some of these issues. It surrounds the base model with scaffolding: specialized sub-agents, a persistent knowledge base, and mechanisms for context management[3][4]. For instance, Amplifier automatically captures and restores the conversation transcript so that long sessions don’t cause the model to lose its mind when context length limits are hit[4]. This could directly combat context rot and ensure consistency in deep chaining. Its sub-agents decompose tasks (e.g. an architecture agent, a debugging agent, etc.), which means a complex recursive task might be split among focused units or tackled iteratively with fresh context at each sub-step rather than one gigantic prompt[27]. Amplifier’s philosophy of “metacognitive recipes” – instructing the AI to plan, verify, test, and correct itself as an expert would[28][29] – aligns with the strategies needed to handle recursion safely (e.g. checking intermediate results, using tools for subtasks, and not over-relying on the model’s memory). Given these advantages, we expect an Amplifier-enhanced model to perform more robustly on recursive challenges than a vanilla LLM, which motivates building a benchmark to quantitatively verify this.
Existing Benchmarks and Methods for Recursive Reasoning
Algorithmic Recursion Benchmarks (Code Generation)
In code generation, recursion is central to many classic problems. Several standard benchmarks include tasks that inherently require recursive solutions: for example, HumanEval (164 coding problems) and MBPP (500 Python puzzles) feature prompts like “Write a function to compute the nth Fibonacci number” or “Traverse a binary tree in-order”[30]. These benchmarks evaluate functional correctness by running the model’s generated code against unit tests. The primary metric is pass@k – the probability that at least one of the model’s $k$ attempts passes all tests[30][31]. HumanEval in particular has become a de facto standard for code-capable LLMs, with pass@1 scores often used to compare models[32][33]. While HumanEval/MBPP cover a range of difficulties, they do include straightforward recursive tasks (factorial, Fibonacci, list reversal, etc.) that are useful sanity checks. Most models today can solve the very basic recursion prompts (GPT-4, for instance, aces Fibonacci and factorial). The real challenge is more complex or deeper recursion – e.g., writing a correct quicksort (which is recursive) or solving recursive dynamic programming problems. The APPS dataset (a large set of competitive programming problems) and Google’s CodeContests include harder recursive algorithms (like backtracking puzzles, DFS on graphs, etc.)[34]. These are typically beyond simple unit tests – they require handling edge cases and deeper reasoning about the algorithm. Current code LLMs achieve relatively low success rates on the hardest APPS problems[35][36], indicating limitations in reliably generating complex recursive logic.
It’s worth noting that evaluation harnesses for code are well-established. For instance, OpenAI’s evaluation of Codex introduced the pass@k metric with code execution[30], and Google’s recent benchmarks use similar execute-and-verify approaches. We will leverage such harness techniques (running generated code in a sandbox to verify outputs) in our benchmark design. One limitation in existing code benchmarks is that they don’t explicitly isolate recursion as a capability – they measure general coding skill. Our eval will specifically target recursion by including multiple tasks that require recursion to solve, and by varying the input size or recursion depth to probe model limits. We will also examine hallucinations in code: e.g., does the model invent nonexistent API calls or produce logically inconsistent code when trying to implement recursion? This kind of error can be seen as a form of hallucination specific to code (producing code that looks plausible but is incorrect). By collecting tasks of different recursion depth and analyzing where models fail (do they get the base case wrong? infinite recursion? off-by-one errors?), we can gain insight into their recursion competency beyond a single scalar score.
Recursive Reasoning & Planning Benchmarks
Outside of pure coding, researchers have crafted benchmarks to test multi-step logical reasoning, some of which inherently involve recursive or hierarchical thinking. A prominent example is the BIG-Bench Hard (BBH) set – 23 challenging tasks from the BIG-Bench benchmark that were identified as especially difficult for models[37]. These include things like multi-step arithmetic, compositional logic puzzles, and temporal reasoning. Chain-of-thought prompting enabled large models (like PaLM) to surpass average human performance on nearly half of these tasks[38]. However, BBH tasks typically involve at most a few layers of reasoning (e.g., a three-step math problem or a short logic puzzle). They don’t push into deep recursion territory; rather, they test whether the model can handle some intermediate steps at all.
For truly recursive reasoning evaluation, researchers have turned to puzzle environments and planning tasks. The Apple AI study mentioned above is one such approach: they used puzzles like Tower of Hanoi, River Crossing problems, Blocks World, etc., where difficulty can be scaled by increasing the number of elements (disks, people, blocks)[39][40]. These puzzles require an agent to generate a sequence of moves or subgoals – essentially a recursive plan – to reach a solution. The evaluation not only checked final correctness (was the puzzle solved?) but also validated each step of the solution using a simulator[41][42]. This step-by-step validation is important; a model might output a long plan where one wrong move invalidates the whole solution. By analyzing the reasoning traces, the study revealed that models often explore correct and incorrect paths in tandem – sometimes finding the right solution but continuing past it or backtracking inefficiently[43][44]. This suggests that current LLMs don’t truly understand the recursive structure of these problems; they are engaging in a kind of trial-and-error simulation in their chain-of-thought, rather than executing a reliably correct recursive algorithm to solve the puzzle. It also highlights that beyond a certain complexity, even providing the model with the known optimal algorithm didn’t help – pointing to a failure to follow long logical chains exactly[19].
Another relevant benchmark domain is long-horizon planning in interactive environments. For example, ALFWorld and the more recent Robotouille environment (a virtual cooking task) have been used to test LLM-based agents on multi-step plans with conditional branching[45][5]. In these benchmarks, an agent must achieve a goal by breaking it into subgoals and reacting to environment feedback. Hierarchical approaches like ReCAP (Recursive Context-Aware Planning) have been proposed to tackle these: ReCAP explicitly implements a recursive decomposition where the model plans ahead, then enters a subtask, then returns and refines the plan, and so on[12][46]. In their experiments, this recursive agent framework greatly improved success rates – for instance, a 32% gain on a synchronous Robotouille task compared to sequential prompting[5]. This indicates that explicit recursive reasoning frameworks can yield more coherent long-term behavior. While such embodied planning tasks are complex to include in a simple eval (they require simulators and possibly human feedback to validate), the ideas from them inform our benchmark design. We aim to include text-based analogs of hierarchical planning tasks. For example, we might present a model with a high-level goal and require it to produce a structured plan (sub-goals and steps), possibly with contingencies – then evaluate the plan’s correctness. Success can be measured by whether following the plan would achieve the goal (this might be checked by a simple script or logical rules for that scenario).
Logical recursion can also appear in purely textual QA tasks. Consider a question that requires reasoning about nested conditions or multi-level inheritance (e.g., a family tree question: “X is the parent of Y, Y is the parent of Z, so what is X to Z?”). Solving this is essentially a recursive traversal of a relationship graph. Datasets in the logical deduction genre (some Big-Bench tasks, or custom puzzles) sometimes include such queries. Another angle is mathematical induction style problems or puzzles like evaluating expressions with deeply nested parentheses – the latter forces the model to apply operations recursively from the innermost layer outwards. While straightforward for a program or a human following PEMDAS rules, LLMs can lose track if the expression is very deeply nested or long. An evaluation might involve asking the model to simplify or evaluate expressions of increasing depth to see where it fails.
Methods to Mitigate Recursive Failures
Before outlining the new benchmark, it’s useful to note existing methods that inspired our approach. One category is using external tools or self-calls to assist the model. Recursive prompting is a novel technique where the model’s output is fed back as input (potentially multiple times) until a condition is met. Andy Konwinski’s Fibonacci experiment is a simple example of this in action: the prompt instructed the model to output the next step of the computation as a new prompt, thereby creating a loop until the base case is reached[47][48]. This approach effectively transforms the LLM into a stateful recursive function operating on its own textual output. However, as noted, it requires careful prompt design and still suffers if the model’s memorized knowledge is imperfect (the model might know the concept of Fibonacci but not compute each value flawlessly)[7]. Handling when the model is wrong – perhaps by having a verification step at each iteration – is an open problem[9]. In our eval, we incorporate scenarios that allow the model to “call itself,” and we test whether Amplifier’s kernel can catch mistakes by, say, running a calculation in a Python module or querying a knowledge base mid-iteration.
Another promising direction is the idea of Recursive Language Models (RLMs)[49]. An RLM treats the model not as a one-shot predictor but as an agent that can spawn smaller sub-calls to itself to handle parts of the input. Zhang et al. (2025) demonstrated this by having a GPT-5 model use a Python REPL: the large input text was stored in the environment, and the model could recursively prompt itself on segments of that text, then combine the results[50][51]. The effect was an illusion of an infinite context window, enabling the model to process very long documents without suffering context rot[10][52]. The RLM approach achieved over double the number of correct answers on a long-context QA benchmark (OOLONG) compared to a standard GPT-5, all while not increasing the overall token usage by much[53][54]. This dramatic gain underscores how recursion (in this case, recursive calls on subsets of input) can be a force multiplier for LLMs when used strategically. We expect Amplifier’s architecture – which similarly can orchestrate multiple model calls and retain state – to benefit from such strategies.
Figure: A conceptual illustration of a Recursive Language Model (RLM). Instead of a single monolithic prompt, the model is given an environment (like a notebook or REPL) where it can recursively call instances of itself on smaller parts of the problem. This provides the user with the illusion of a near-infinite context window, as the model partitions the input and processes it in pieces to avoid context degradation[55]. Such RLM strategies have been shown to mitigate long-context “rot” and significantly improve accuracy on very long inputs[53]. Our evaluation will include tasks that encourage models to use similar recursive decomposition (either via Amplifier’s tools or via multi-step prompting) to see if this improves outcomes.[49][52]
Tool use and self-correction are also crucial. Amplifier already encourages generating tests and verifying outputs as part of its workflow (e.g. “implement → test → fix” loops[56]). For recursive problems, an ideal system might generate a draft solution for a sub-problem, test it (perhaps by executing code or checking logical constraints), then only proceed if the test passes – akin to how a human would ensure each recursive step is correct. OpenAI’s GPT-4 has demonstrated some ability to self-debug code (by running it and reading error messages), and approaches like Ghostwriter’s “critic” mode or Self-Critique prompting get the model to identify flaws in its answers. Our benchmark plans to measure whether a model (or Amplifier with its modules) can recognize and correct mistakes in a recursive context. For instance, if the model’s first attempt at a recursive function fails a test, does it autonomously adjust its approach? If a multi-step reasoning yields a contradiction, does the system notice and revise an earlier assumption? These behaviors would earn credit in our eval scoring.
In summary, existing benchmarks and research give us the building blocks: unit-test-based code evaluations, logical puzzles and planning tasks, and methods like recursive prompting, chain-of-thought, and self-reflection. We will now synthesize these into a unified evaluation suite specifically aimed at recursive capabilities, and design it to be runnable and extensible within the Amplifier framework or any standard eval harness.
Benchmark Design for Recursive Capabilities in Amplifier
Our proposed benchmark is organized into three categories of recursive tasks: (1) Algorithmic recursion in code, (2) Recursive reasoning and planning, and (3) Deep chaining and self-referential prompts. Together, these cover a spectrum from classical programming recursion to abstract logical recursion. For each category, we define example tasks, the evaluation method (how we’ll run tests or check answers), and the specific aspects of recursion being measured (correctness, depth handling, hallucination mitigation, etc.). The benchmark is implemented using a flexible evaluation harness – we suggest using the EleutherAI LM Evaluation Harness (an open-source framework for evaluating LLMs[57]), extended with custom code for interactive prompting as needed. This choice is intentional: the harness provides a clear structure for adding new tasks and models, aligning with the requirement to be easy to deploy and extend. (Alternately, one could use OpenAI’s evals library or even custom test scripts; our provided code scaffolds can be adapted to either.) The Amplifier team can integrate this by treating each eval task as an “Amplifier scenario” – essentially a scripted sequence of prompts and checks that can run using Amplifier’s stable kernel and toolset.
Category 1: Algorithmic Recursion Tasks (Code Generation)
Goal: Evaluate the model’s ability to implement and execute recursive algorithms correctly in code. This tests both the model’s understanding of the recursive logic and its propensity to hallucinate or err in code syntax/semantics. We will measure functional correctness (does the code produce the expected output for test cases?) and how the model handles increasing recursion depth (e.g., performance on simple vs more complex inputs).
Example tasks:
- Fibonacci and Factorial: Prompt the model to write a function (in Python) that computes fib(n) or fact(n) recursively. These are simple but allow testing larger n for depth. For instance, we might test the function on n=5, 10, 20. A correct model should easily handle these (and perhaps memoize or at least not timeout on n=20). We check that outputs match the known values. Any deviation is a failure on correctness. We also watch for efficiency or other issues (if a model writes an exponential-time solution for fib, that’s expected here, but if it times out or runs into recursion limits, that’s noted).
- Tree Traversal: e.g., “Given a binary tree node class, write a function to perform inorder traversal of the tree and return the sequence of values.” This requires a recursive tree walk. The eval can construct a simple binary tree in code and verify that the model’s function returns nodes in the correct order. We include edge cases like a single-node tree, an unbalanced tree, etc. This tests whether the model can handle recursive data structures and base cases properly.
- Backtracking puzzle: e.g., “Solve the N-Queens problem for a given N and return one valid arrangement.” or “Generate all permutations of a list of numbers.” These are classic recursive/backtracking tasks. We would likely constrain N small (e.g., N=4 for queens) to keep runtime reasonable. The correctness check is either via known solutions or checking solution validity (e.g., no two queens attack each other). This pushes the model a bit more – it has to manage more complex recursion and possibly prune search. It’s also a place where hallucination might occur (the model might produce an incomplete solution or a wrong format).
Evaluation method: For each problem, we have a reference solution or an oracle (for simple numeric tasks, we can compute the true answer; for others, we have validators). We will use the harness to prompt the model with the problem description and function signature, then take its completion as code. Using an isolated Python execution environment, we’ll run the model’s code against a set of test cases. In code, this looks like:
# Pseudocode for code execution eval
from model_api import generate_code  # imaginary interface to the model
import importlib.util, types

problem_prompt = "'''Implement a function fib(n) that returns the nth Fibonacci number...'''"
generated_code = generate_code(problem_prompt, model="Amplifier-LLM")
# Save code to a temporary file/module
with open("temp_solution.py", "w") as f:
    f.write(generated_code)
# Dynamically import the module and get the function
spec = importlib.util.spec_from_file_location("temp_solution", "temp_solution.py")
module = importlib.util.module_from_spec(spec)
spec.loader.exec_module(module)
# Run test cases
tests = {0:0, 1:1, 5:5, 10:55, 15:610}
passed = 0
for inp, expected in tests.items():
    try:
        result = module.fib(inp)
    except Exception as e:
        result = e  # capture exceptions as failures
    if result == expected:
        passed += 1
score = passed / len(tests)
print(f"Fib function correctness: {score*100:.1f}%")
(The above is a simplified illustration; the actual harness integration would handle multiple samples, cleanup, etc.)
We will gather metrics like Pass@1 (did the first attempt pass all tests?), and possibly Pass@3 or @5 if we allow multiple tries. We also log failure modes: Did the code raise a recursion depth error (if so, the model might not have used an iterative solution where needed)? Did it produce a plausible-looking but logically wrong output (indicating a hallucinated approach)? For Amplifier specifically, we can compare runs with and without Amplifier’s modules. For instance, Amplifier might use its testing agent to catch an error and regenerate – so maybe the first attempt fails but it self-corrects. We would give partial credit in the eval if the system eventually produces a working solution via such self-correction. The harness can accommodate this by allowing the model to take multiple turns (though the standard LM eval treats each attempt independently, we might script a custom evaluation loop for Amplifier that mimics its iterative development style).
Expected outcomes: Modern code-focused LLMs should score high on basic tasks like Fibonacci (nearly 100% Pass@1 for GPT-4 level)[33], but might struggle on more complex ones like N-Queens or novel recursive puzzles. We expect Amplifier’s approach (with built-in testing and debugging) to improve reliability – e.g., if the first output is wrong, Amplifier might notice a failed test and prompt the model to fix the code, resulting in a correct solution on a second attempt. We will score such a scenario as a success (since the end-to-end system succeeded without human intervention). The scoring methodology here will primarily be percentage of tasks solved correctly (each task might have multiple test cases, all must pass for success). We might also record the average attempts needed when using Amplifier (a lower is better). For hallucination tracking, any generated code that includes nonsensical API calls or irrelevant operations can be flagged. For example, if a model randomly imports modules or defines unused variables (common signs of confused generation), we note that as a minor quality issue, though the main metric is still functional correctness.
Category 2: Recursive Reasoning & Planning Tasks
Goal: Test the model’s ability to handle problems that require hierarchical reasoning or multi-step planning, which often have a recursive structure. We measure correctness of final answers or plans, coherence of intermediate reasoning, and the ability to handle increased complexity (more nested steps or deeper inference chains) without losing consistency. We also explicitly assess hallucination in the reasoning: does the model introduce facts or steps that are not supported?
Example tasks:
- Tower of Hanoi (small disks): We will use a textual version of Tower of Hanoi, e.g., “There are 3 disks on peg A, move them to peg C following the rules. Output the sequence of moves.” For 3 disks, the solution is 7 moves (which we can enumerate as the expected answer). For 4 disks, it’s 15 moves. We can include both 3 and 4-disk versions. The model is expected to output a sequence like “Move A->C, Move A->B, …” etc. We check if the sequence is valid and complete. A simple checker can simulate the moves to ensure all disks end on the target peg with no illegal moves. This task directly evaluates recursive planning: the optimal solution follows a recursive pattern. We’ll see if the model can either recall that pattern or derive it. Even if it doesn’t produce the minimal moves but still solves it in the allowed moves, we consider it correct (though extraneous moves could indicate some confusion).
- Nested Logical Queries: For example, “Alice, Bob, and Charlie follow these truths: Alice says ‘Bob always lies’, Bob says ‘Charlie always lies’, Charlie says ‘Alice and Bob lie’. If we know exactly one of them is telling the truth, who is telling the truth?” This kind of puzzle requires considering statements about statements – a recursive logic problem. The model has to resolve it by possibly evaluating one assumption, then another (a form of backtracking in logic). We have a ground-truth answer (say, Alice is telling the truth, as a hypothetical answer) that we compare against. More generally, any riddle or puzzle with nested conditions (like those involving multiple “if” layers) can fit here. We evaluate if the model’s answer is correct and if its explanation (if it provides one) is logically sound. If the model just guesses without explanation, we might prompt it to show reasoning or at least verify the answer with a reasoning chain.
- Multi-step Plan (textual): A task like “Outline a plan to bake a cake and clean the kitchen afterwards, given you have to also go buy ingredients first.” This involves ordering sub-tasks: Buy ingredients -> Bake cake -> Clean kitchen, which is a three-step plan. It’s simple, but we can increase complexity: add more interdependent steps or conditional steps (e.g., “if the store is closed, do X”). The idea is to see if the model can recursively break down a goal into subgoals and list them in a correct sequence. Another example: “Explain how to solve a Rubik’s cube in steps.” While the model likely won’t have the precise algorithm memorized beyond a certain depth (Rubik’s solutions are complex), it might outline high-level recursive strategies (solve one face, then another, etc.). We won’t score on optimality here, just on logical coherence: Does each step follow from the previous? Does the plan achieve the stated goal? Amplifier’s architecture, which favors brainstorm -> plan -> implement -> test workflows[56], should shine in such tasks, because it naturally tries to first produce a structured plan. We will allow multi-turn interactions for Amplifier in these cases (the model might output a plan, then a different module might “execute” a step or verify it).
Evaluation method: For puzzles with objective answers (like logic puzzles or Tower of Hanoi), we have a clear correctness criterion (e.g., correct person identified as truth-teller, or final configuration achieved with legal moves). We will parse the model’s output to extract the answer or the sequence of moves, then use a programmatic checker. For instance, for Hanoi:
moves = model_answer.split('\n')
if simulate_hanoi(moves, n_disks=3) == True:
    score = 1.0  # success
else:
    score = 0.0
The simulate_hanoi function will ensure no move violates rules and that we end up with all disks on the target peg. For the logic puzzle, we can have a small brute-force solver that checks each person’s statements for consistency, but since we know the intended answer, a simpler check is just string matching the correct name (assuming the puzzle isn’t ambiguous). We will still review the chain-of-thought for hallucinations: for example, if the model’s explanation invokes a fact not in the puzzle (like “Charlie lied on Tuesday” when no days were mentioned), that’s a hallucination. We’ll deduct points or flag that. This might be a manual analysis unless we craft puzzles such that any extra proper noun or detail is clearly a hallucination (we could do that).
For plan sequencing tasks, evaluation is a bit more heuristic. We can prepare a list of key steps that any correct plan should include (similar to how some QA benchmarks use rubrics). For the cake example: the plan must mention buying ingredients, then baking (mixing, preheating, etc.), then cleaning up, in a logically feasible order. We can do a keyword check (e.g., “buy” appears before “bake”, “clean” is last). We will also check for catastrophic omissions (if the model forgets to actually bake the cake after buying ingredients, that’s a failure). Because this is somewhat subjective, the scoring will be coarse: full credit if all essential sub-tasks are present and correctly ordered; partial credit if one sub-task is missing or order is slightly off; no credit if the plan is nonsense or fundamentally wrong. We might complement this with model-based evaluation: for example, use another LLM to judge plan quality (this is beyond scope for now, so we stick to rule-based checks).
We will encourage the model to show its reasoning or plan explicitly by phrasing prompts like, “List the steps…” or “Explain your reasoning.” This makes evaluation easier since the output is structured. Amplifier’s sub-agents might internally do reasoning that isn’t all shown, but we can configure it to output its final chain or summary.
Expected outcomes: We anticipate that for simpler instances (Hanoi with 3 disks, basic logic puzzle), strong models will perform well, and Amplifier should too. As we scale up complexity (4 disks, more people in a logic puzzle, more steps in a plan), a vanilla model might begin to falter (e.g., output an incorrect move sequence or get the puzzle wrong), whereas Amplifier might maintain performance by virtue of its structured approach (e.g., its reasoning agent might break the logic puzzle into cases systematically, or its planning agent might ensure no step is skipped). We will measure success rate per task and also look at recursion depth vs success (e.g., % solved for 3-disk vs 4-disk Hanoi). This can reveal how well the system handles increasing depth. If the success drops sharply beyond a point, that point can be considered the system’s effective recursion limit for that type of task. One could imagine plotting this if we had many levels; for now we just report it qualitatively.
We will also record hallucination-like behaviors: e.g., in a plan, did the model insert a step that makes no sense (“teleport to kitchen” as a joke, or referencing a tool not available)? In a reasoning chain, did it assume a false premise? Each such instance will be noted; a robust system should have few to none. Amplifier’s knowledge base might help here by grounding facts, and its insistence on verification might catch inconsistencies, so we expect fewer hallucinated steps with Amplifier. If, for example, Amplifier’s agent asks the model to double-check each move in Hanoi (maybe by simulating internally or recomputing), it could catch a wrong move before presenting the final answer – something a raw LLM wouldn’t do. Those differences will be reflected in the scores and analysis of errors.
Category 3: Deep Chaining & Self-Referential Tests
Goal: Push the model (and system) into recursive self-call scenarios to test stability over long generations. This category evaluates whether the model can remain stable and goal-directed when its output is fed back as new input repeatedly, a situation that can amplify both errors and drift. We measure if the process converges to a correct result, and monitor the evolution of content for drift or nonsense. Essentially, this tests the system’s ability to act like a recursive function or an iterative agent without diverging. It’s especially relevant for Amplifier, which might loop an agent until a condition is met.
Example tests:
- Recursive Chain-of-Thought (self-refinement): We design a prompt that explicitly tells the model to call itself. For instance: “You are a recursive solver. If the problem is not solved, reformulate it and call the solver again. Problem: Compute 4! (4 factorial).” The expected behavior is the model would output something like “Problem is not solved yet. Let’s break it down: Compute 4 * solve 3!. New Problem: Compute 3!.” That new problem (3!) would be fed in as the next input (because we capture the model’s output and loop it). This continues until it hits the base case (1! or 0!), then outputs an answer, and unwinds. In practice, implementing this requires carefully parsing the outputs. We can simplify: instruct the model to output only the next sub-problem or the final answer when done. The evaluation checks that the final answer is correct (24 for 4!). More generally, we can attempt this for n! for various n, or Fibonacci similarly. The key metrics: Does it terminate? (Some models might keep looping or not recognize the base case.) Is each step valid? (No incorrect reformulation or arithmetic error mid-way.) And how many iterations did it take versus optimal? A well-behaved recursion should take n steps for factorial of n, for example. If we see it taking more, it might be circling or doing something odd. We’ll also check for any hallucinated content in intermediate prompts (it should ideally only talk about the subproblem at hand).
- Iterative Summarization for Drift: We take a moderately complex paragraph (e.g., a Wikipedia introduction on a topic) and ask the model to summarize it. Then we feed the summary back and ask for a further summary, and repeat this, say, 5–10 times (or until the summary is just one sentence). We then compare the final summary to the original. We expect some loss of detail (that’s normal), but the core meaning should remain. We will evaluate meaning preservation by checking if certain key information (names, dates, or critical facts) from the original are still present or correctly implied. We can automate a bit: if the original text mentions, say, “Alan Turing” and “Enigma machine”, does the final summary still hint at those? A semantic similarity score or embedding-based comparison could be used as well. Additionally, we examine if the genre or tone drifts. Prior research suggests models might transform text towards more generic or unrelated content under repeated rephrasing[14][58]. We will look for that – e.g., does a technical paragraph devolve into a motivational quote? If Amplifier’s knowledge base is used, it might help retain facts by re-injecting them, so we anticipate Amplifier might maintain fidelity better. The scoring here could be: 1 point for each essential fact preserved, minus points for each major distortion or addition that wasn’t in the original. If factual accuracy stays high but we detect semantic drift (as Maxwell et al. did[1][2]), we will note that specifically. The ideal outcome is the final summary, though short, still answers the question “what was the original about?” correctly.
- Auto-regressive Reasoning Loop: This scenario mimics an “AutoGPT”-style agent: the model is given an open-ended task (e.g., “Find a prime number larger than 500 that is palindromic”) and it is allowed to generate a plan, execute steps, and call itself until done. We can simulate this by a prompt pattern where the model outputs an action and a thought, and the harness either executes the action or, if the action is just “think”, feeds back the thought. For example, the model might say: “Thought: I should check primes one by one. Action: test 503.” The harness could check if 503 is prime (using a simple function) and return the result to the model (like “503 is prime, task complete”). If the model instead tries a wrong approach or loops poorly, that will show in the number of iterations or a failure to stop. We measure success if it finds a correct answer and stops in a reasonable number of steps. This kind of eval is more complex but demonstrates Amplifier’s agent loop capabilities in a measurable way.
Evaluation method: For the explicit recursion prompts (factorial, etc.), we will implement a driver that feeds the model’s output back in repeatedly. The harness can use a simple loop as illustrated below:
prompt = "You are a recursive solver. Provide next sub-problem or FINAL answer.\nProblem: Compute 4!"
for iteration in range(20):  # safety limit
    response = model.generate(prompt)
    print(f"Iteration {iteration}: {response}")
    if "FINAL" in response:
        answer = extract_answer(response)  # parse the final answer
        break
    else:
        # Prepare the next prompt from model's response
        prompt = response  # (assuming response is already phrased as next problem)
We’ll have to craft the prompt and extraction carefully. The score is binary: correct final answer or not. We’ll also log the number of iterations and any anomalies (e.g., if it hits the limit without producing a FINAL, that’s a failure). For summarization, the harness simply calls the model repeatedly with “Summarize the above text further.” The final output is compared to reference important points. We might manually identify the key points for a few test texts and code a check for them. For example, if the original says “Alan Turing was a mathematician who broke the Enigma”, our check could be to see if the final summary still contains “Alan Turing” (or at least “Turing”) and “Enigma” or synonyms. If one or both are missing or altered incorrectly, that’s drift. We can quantify drift as 0 (no drift, all key info present), 0.5 (some info lost but main idea intact), 1 (main idea lost or replaced). Lower is better in terms of drift.
To evaluate hallucination mitigation, this category is key. When the model rephrases text iteratively, does it start injecting new untrue statements? If yes, that’s factual hallucination. Semantic drift (changing context to another domain) is another form of unintended generation. Our scoring will separate these: factual accuracy after each generation (which tends to remain high per Maxwell’s finding[1]) and semantic fidelity. If Amplifier uses retrieval or its knowledge base at each step, it might actually re-inject facts that could counteract drift (or it might not drift in the first place). We’ll observe the difference by running the same test with and without Amplifier’s augmentation.
Expected outcomes: In a well-designed system, the factorial or Fibonacci recursive prompt loop should converge to the correct answer every time for reasonable n (these are simple tasks). If a model fails here, it indicates it either didn’t follow instructions or can’t do basic arithmetic – unlikely for top models. However, where we might see differences is efficiency and clarity. A weaker model might add unnecessary commentary each iteration or require more iterations. Amplifier could enforce a cleaner format via prompt policy, making the loop very direct. For summarization, we expect to see some degree of drift in any vanilla LLM if we go many rounds (as the study suggests[14]). The Amplifier-enhanced model might retain the structure better (perhaps by recognizing the task and using a built-in summarization tool or by deliberately preserving context). We will measure how much of the original meaning is preserved – ideally Amplifier’s final summaries will score closer to the original on content preservation.
If the drift study result holds, we might confirm that factual content stays high but the framing shifts – for example, final summaries often turning business- or advice-oriented regardless of input domain[59]. Our eval would catch that by noticing the appearance of generic phrases. If Amplifier’s “kernel” mode has any guardrails against style drift, we’ll see a difference in the final style.
Implementation and Harness Integration
We emphasize that this benchmark is fully runnable. Each task can be encoded as part of an evaluation suite. Using the LM Evaluation Harness, we can create a new task class for each category or even each task type. For instance, a RecursionFibTask that implements prompt() and verify() methods, or a unified RecursiveEval task that covers all and sums the results. The harness supports both generative evaluations (checking if output text contains the correct answer) and programmatic evaluations (like running code or custom logic). We will utilize both modes. The code tasks use programmatic evaluation (running the code), while reasoning tasks sometimes use text matching or small simulations.

The harness can then aggregate, say, the average accuracy across all instances. For code tasks, a Task class might compile and run the code as shown previously.
Modularity: New tasks can be added by simply creating more instances or classes. For example, if we want to add a new recursive math puzzle, we add a new document in the dataset or a new method in the class. The benchmark is easy to extend: one can plug in a different model (just point the harness to a different model API or weights) and all these tasks can be rerun. It’s also straightforward to add more test cases or increase difficulty – e.g., test factorial of 20 instead of 10.
Running under Amplifier: Amplifier could be treated as an LLM endpoint that internally does multi-step processing. Alternatively, we might run the model in Amplifier’s own evaluation mode. The key is that our eval definitions focus on inputs and final outputs. If Amplifier internally uses agents, as long as from the outside we get an answer or solution, we can evaluate it in the same way. We might need to allow the model more time or iterations for Amplifier runs, since it might do a plan-implement cycle internally. That’s fine – we can increase timeouts or number of tokens allowed for those runs. The harness supports settings like that.
Expected Outcomes and Scoring Methodology
After running the benchmark, we will have detailed results per task. We will report metrics such as:
Code Recursion Score: e.g., 80% (4 out of 5 code tasks passed all tests). We may break this down by task (Fib: 100%, Tree: 100%, Permutations: 60%, etc.). We also note if any tasks needed multiple tries under Amplifier (which indicates self-correction was used successfully).
Reasoning/Planning Score: e.g., 70% of reasoning tasks answered correctly. We’ll highlight which ones failed – perhaps the 5-disk Hanoi was too hard or a particular logic puzzle stumped the model. Any systematic hallucination (like always adding an extra step in plans) will be described.
Stability/Chaining Score: This is less about a single number and more about observations: e.g., “Factorial self-call succeeded for n up to 10, but model got stuck in a loop for n=15” or “Summarization drift: on average 2 key facts dropped by iteration 5, purpose fidelity degraded in 3/5 trials.” If we wanted a single number, we could craft something like “Average semantic similarity after 5 iterations” as a percentage. But qualitative analysis is important here.
We will also compare Model vs Amplifier if possible. For example:
Vanilla GPT-4: solves 3-disk Hanoi but makes an error on 4-disk; Amplifier (GPT-4 with modules) solves both correctly due to better planning. We’d note that improvement.
In summarization, maybe vanilla GPT-4 final summary lost 2 key points, while Amplifier’s summary (perhaps using its knowledge base to re-check context) lost only 1 or none. That indicates better preservation.
Scoring methodology: Each task or sub-task can be given a weight (equal weight for simplicity). The simplest aggregate is overall accuracy (% of tasks solved). However, since we have different facets, we might report a multi-metric evaluation:
Correctness Score: percentage of tasks with correct final outputs (code outputs correct, answers correct, etc.).
Hallucination Score: perhaps measured inversely as the percentage of tasks with no hallucination observed. (If the model hallucinated in 2 out of 10 tasks, then 80% no-hallucination). Lower hallucination is better. This might be a bit subjective for reasoning tasks, but we will use concrete signals as discussed (e.g., ungrounded facts).
Depth Robustness: we could define this as the deepest level at which the model maintained correctness. For example, “Hanoi-depth = 3 (solved 3 disks, failed 4 disks)”, “Summarization-depth = 7 iterations before meaning lost”. We can list these or turn them into a score by normalizing against some target. But listing is more informative.

Prioritizing ease of deployment: All tasks are designed to run with automated checking – no human evaluation needed in the loop. We chose well-defined problems or created deterministic checkers for open tasks. This means Amplifier’s team (or anyone) can run this benchmark repeatedly to track progress. As new modules are added to Amplifier, the eval can quickly show if, say, the new “planner agent” improved the planning task scores, or if a better “self-debug” routine improved code recursion tasks. The modular design also means one can test various model backends (swap Claude for GPT, etc.) under the same scenarios to see which model handles recursion best, all within Amplifier or externally.
Conclusion
Evaluating recursive capabilities in LLMs requires going beyond one-shot Q&A – it demands assessing how a model or agent behaves in multi-step, self-referential workflows. We have compiled existing insights and resources on this front: from code benchmarks (HumanEval, APPS) that contain recursive problems, to novel research on long-chain reasoning, planning, and iterative prompting. Building on these, we proposed a comprehensive benchmark for Amplifier that tests recursion in coding, reasoning, and iterative self-calls. The benchmark is designed to be runnable and extensible, leveraging a standard evaluation harness and simple pass/fail criteria for each step. It emphasizes not just whether the model can get the right answer, but how it gets there – tracking correctness at depth, incidence of hallucination, and stability of its reasoning process under prolonged chaining. By running this suite, the Amplifier team can quantify the benefits of its approach: for example, demonstrating that Amplifier’s structured agents yield fewer errors on deep recursion tasks or that its context management prevents the semantic drift seen in vanilla LLMs[15].
Ultimately, this eval benchmark will serve as a regression test for recursion: as Amplifier’s modules improve, the expectation is that scores on these tasks will improve correspondingly. New types of recursive challenges can be added easily, ensuring the evaluation stays relevant. In a broader sense, we hope this encourages the community to include recursive reasoning in LLM assessment – an area crucial for advanced AI systems (especially those that aim to “think” in multi-step workflows or serve as autonomous agents). By clearly defining the hypothesis and measuring outcomes in a reproducible way, we make progress toward LLMs that don’t just solve problems in one shot, but can reliably handle problems that solve sub-problems of themselves, much like a human engineer or planner would. The path to such systems will be iterative (and recursive!) – and with the benchmark outlined here, we can track that journey with empirical evidence.
